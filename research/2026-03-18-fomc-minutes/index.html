FOMC Minutes — March 17–18, 2026

March 18, 20263 min readOpen in the reader

Decision

Under Chair Jerome Powell, the Committee again held the federal funds range at 3.50–3.75%. IORB stayed at 3.65%, and the primary credit and standing repo rates at 3.75%. The vote was 11–1. Waller rejoined the majority, and Stephen Miran was the only dissenter, preferring a quarter-point cut. He argued that "the current stance of policy remained restrictive and was contributing to weak labor demand." This was a projections meeting. The SEP was released separately and its figures do not appear in the minutes.

Economic outlook

The staff forecast was weaker than in January, reflecting incoming data and less support from financial conditions. GDP was now expected to grow roughly in line with potential through 2028. Unemployment was seen holding near its current level through most of next year before edging down to the natural rate. Staff saw risks to growth and employment tilted to the downside.

Inflation

Progress on inflation had stalled. Headline PCE was 2.8% in January and core PCE was 3.1%. Staff estimated February at 2.8% headline and 3.0% core. Tariffs kept core goods inflation elevated, and nonhousing services remained high. Near-term inflation expectations rose along with oil prices. The vast majority of participants judged that the risk of inflation staying persistently above target had increased. Staff still expected inflation to come back close to 2% by the end of next year as energy and tariff effects faded.

Labor market

Unemployment was 4.4% in February, and job gains stayed low. Most participants saw the labor market as broadly in balance, with slow hiring reflecting slower labor force growth. Others pointed to softening signs, such as a small rise in prime-age unemployment and job gains concentrated in a few sectors. The vast majority saw downside risks to employment. Many noted firms might hold off on hiring as they adopt AI.

Financial markets & balance sheet

Oil futures jumped about 50% over the intermeeting period. Longer-dated contracts rose much less, which suggested the spike might be temporary. Stocks fell about 5%, with software hit hardest on fears of AI disruption. Short-term Treasury yields rose. Futures no longer fully priced a cut before December, and options implied roughly a 30% chance of a hike early next year. Redemption requests at private credit funds rose notably. Reserve management purchases continued. Reserves were expected to bottom in late April and then average about $3 trillion through September, with the pace of purchases slowing significantly after April.

Risks participants flagged

  • A drawn-out Middle East conflict that raises energy prices, feeds into core inflation, slows hiring and hurts growth abroad
  • Inflation staying high longer, with long-term expectations becoming more sensitive to energy prices
  • AI disruption to business models and jobs
  • Uncertainty over when tariff effects would fade

What it signaled about next moves

Almost everyone supported holding, seeing policy as within the range of plausible neutral estimates. Many still expected cuts over time if inflation declined, but a couple pushed their expected timing further out. Some favored two-sided guidance that allowed for hikes. Most said it was too early to judge the Middle East effects. Policy would be set meeting by meeting.

Read-through for SPY

  • The oil shock and stalled inflation pushed rate cuts further away. Markets went from expecting cuts to putting about 30% odds on a hike, a clear headwind for stock valuations.
  • The roughly 5% drop in stocks over the period was concentrated in software on AI-disruption fears, which points to sector rotation as well as broad pressure.
  • Rising private credit redemptions were a credit-market crack worth watching.
