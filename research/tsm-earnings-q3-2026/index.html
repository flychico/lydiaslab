TSM earnings — Taiwan Semiconductor, Q3 2026

October 15, 202613 min readOpen in the reader

Management sees AI chip demand still above what it can make, and the thing that could spoil it is spending at record levels for a boom whose length it will not vouch for itself.

Reported 2026-10-15. Next report: not found (last year's fourth-quarter report came in mid-January; monthly sales are next due Dec 10). Updated 2026-11-12. Research, not investment advice.

The short version

  • Demand: stronger. AI demand is still above supply; October revenue was up 59.7% from a year ago.
  • Guidance: raised. 2026 growth in US dollars about 44%, from slightly above 40%. Q4 revenue US$52.3B–$54.3B. Capital spending held at US$60B–$64B.
  • Biggest worry: how long AI demand lasts against the spending. Management calls its 2027 planning prudent and gave no 2027 figures.
  • Chart: leans up, weakly. Price closed $413.35, above the 200-day ($401.05). A close below $401.05 undoes it.

The numbers

  • Revenue US$46.72B, up 41.2% in US dollars, vs $45.09B estimated, and above the top of its own US$44.6B–$45.8B guide. (Webull shows $46.17B, which looks like a currency conversion difference.)
  • EPS US$4.60 per ADR vs $4.51 estimated. Gross margin 66.4%.
  • October revenue, reported Nov 10: NT$586.89B, up 59.7% from a year ago.

What management said about the road ahead

  • Next quarter: revenue US$52.3B–$54.3B, gross margin 65%–67%, operating margin 56.5%–58.5%.
  • Demand: AI demand is still above supply. It would not say whether the gap is widening or narrowing.
  • New process: Q4 is driven by leading-edge demand, including a steep ramp of its newest 2-nanometer process.
  • Margins: gross margin fell in Q3 mainly because the new process earns less at first. Overseas plants take about 2 points off margin in 2026, widening to 3–4 points later.
  • Mix: high-performance computing, which includes AI, was 68% of revenue; North American customers were 79%.
  • 2027: planning is described as prudent. No 2027 revenue or spending figure.
  • In their words: "we expect our business to be supported by continued strong demand." (Wendell Huang, CFO)

What management is worried about

  1. Weak consumer and price-sensitive markets. Rising component prices are squeezing phones and PCs, the other 32% of the business.
  2. Margin dilution. The 2-nanometer ramp now, and overseas plants costing 2 points in 2026 and 3–4 points later.
  3. Tight equipment supply. It limits how fast new capacity arrives.
  4. Power for US data centers. If customers cannot power their sites, they cannot take the chips.
  5. How far to commit. Spending in 2026–2028 is expected to be significantly higher than in the prior three years, yet 2027 is planned prudently.
  6. Tariffs were not discussed on the call. The annual report's risk section was not opened, so Taiwan and geopolitical wording is not found here.

The big worry: how long AI demand lasts against record spending, both ways

What would ease it

  • Customers keep committing years ahead. Nvidia says supply stays its bottleneck through at least the end of fiscal 2028; AMD raised its 2027 growth view to over 80%; Broadcom says supply is secured for fiscal 2027 and 2028.
  • Monthly sales keep running at October's pace (up 59.7%). The next figure is due Dec 10.
  • TSMC gives a 2027 spending figure in January at or above this year's US$60B–$64B. That would be management vouching for the demand.
  • Power and equipment bottlenecks loosen, so chips already ordered can be installed.

What would make it worse

  • AI buyers' credit tightens. The Fed is expected to hike in December (about 90% odds), the 10-year is near 5%, and Governor Bowman has flagged over $800B of AI debt and circular financing.
  • Customers do not build all they announced. Broadcom itself cautioned that not all announced capacity is guaranteed to be built.
  • Consumer weakness deepens. AMD and Micron both expect PC and phone units to fall by double digits.
  • Overseas plants dilute margin faster than the 3–4 points flagged, just as nearly 20 plants are in progress.

How this company is placed against it

  • Pricing power: it makes the chips for Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom, and about 95% of Broadcom's wafers. Gross margin was 66.4% even in a ramp quarter.
  • It is the cautious one. It held capital spending while raising growth, and its 2027 wording is the most reserved in this group. If demand slows, it has committed less than its customers have.
  • Concentration cuts both ways: 79% of revenue is North American, 68% is high-performance computing. It is a bet on the same few buyers as everyone else here.

Money plans

  • Capital spending: US$13.84B in Q3, US$40.64B so far this year; 2026 held at US$60B–$64B. 2026–2028 expected to be significantly higher than the prior three years.
  • Plants: nearly 20 in progress; the second Arizona plant is due in volume in Q3 2027.
  • Dividend: NT$7.00 a share for Q2.
  • Buybacks, debt, hiring: not found.

People and governance

  • CEO: C.C. Wei, CEO since June 2018 and also chairman since June 2024. Not the founder (that was Morris Chang); he joined in 1998.
  • CFO: Wendell Huang, senior vice president and spokesperson.
  • Successor named: not found. The annual report chapter read has no succession statement.
  • Chair and CEO: combined in one person since June 2024.
  • Control: one class of common stock, no preferred. Taiwan's National Development Fund holds 6.38% (as of Dec 17, 2025). No controlling holder, but the government is the largest named holder after the ADR depositary (20.49%).
  • Recent changes or investigations: former senior vice president Wei-Jen Lo retired in July 2025 and joined Intel that October. TSMC sued him on Nov 25, 2025 over a non-compete and trade secrets. Liberty Times, as relayed by TrendForce, reported that investigators searched his home and seized materials; Intel's CEO called the allegations without merit. These are reported allegations, not findings. Any change since then: not found.
  • How much rests on one person: moderate. Wei holds both top jobs with no named successor, but the chair has already changed hands twice since 2018 (founder Morris Chang to Mark Liu, then Liu to Wei).
