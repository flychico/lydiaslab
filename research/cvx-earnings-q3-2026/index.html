CVX earnings — Chevron, Q3 2026

October 30, 202613 min readOpen in the reader

Management sees a supply-constrained market keeping prices and margins high into next year, and the thing that could spoil it is oil falling back once Middle East flows return.

Reported 2026-10-30. Next report: not found. Updated 2026-11-12. Research, not investment advice.

The short version

  • Demand: Stronger. Refining and petrochemical margins are elevated, with about 10% of world refining capacity offline.
  • Guidance: Held, trending low on spending. Capex to finish at the low end of $18–19B. Q4 buybacks $2.5–3.0B, the same range as earlier quarters.
  • Biggest worry: The oil price coming back down. Each $1 of Brent is about $600M a year after tax.
  • Chart: No lean. A daily close above the 50-day at $215.22 leans up; a daily close under $208.55 leans down.

The numbers

  • GAAP EPS $5.06, adjusted $5.28. Estimates were $4.85 (Webull) or $4.78 (Earnings Whispers). A beat on both bases.
  • Total revenues and other income $67.8B against $62.0B (Webull).
  • Nine-month production averaged 3,970 thousand barrels a day, just under the 3,980–4,100 full-year range. Whether the range was formally changed: not found.

What management said about the road ahead

  • Prices: Crude ranged from $68 to $132 inside the quarter. Brent was about $96 and Henry Hub gas about $2.63 in early November (10-Q).
  • Refining: About 10% of global capacity is offline. Margins should stay elevated near term, then ease.
  • Petrochemicals: Elevated through year-end and perhaps into 2027, with a warning of oversupply when flows normalise.
  • Supply: International production fell 109 thousand barrels a day on lower entitlements at higher prices and the Middle East conflict. Physical operations are not materially affected so far; the situation is called volatile.
  • Permian and Venezuela: Permian held above 1 million barrels of oil equivalent a day. The Venezuela joint ventures plan $7B over five years to roughly double output to about 600 thousand barrels a day by 2031.
  • Power: A 2.67 GW West Texas power project has a 20-year contract with Microsoft, first power in 2028. The final investment decision is still pending.
  • In his words: "We're reinvesting to bring more energy to a supply-constrained market." (Mike Wirth, Chairman and CEO)

What management is worried about

  1. The Middle East conflict. Curtailments in the Partitioned Zone and at the Saudi and Qatar chemical ventures; international output down 109 thousand barrels a day.
  2. Price sensitivity. About $600M a year after tax for each $1 move in Brent.
  3. Hess Midstream deal. The one new risk factor in the 10-Q: it could be delayed or fail. It is due to close by year-end with a one-time non-cash loss of about $3–4B.
  4. Petrochemical oversupply once flows normalise. No figure given.
  5. Caspian pipeline route. The filing warns that attacks on it could hurt, and cites higher shipping and insurance costs. No figure given.

The big worry: oil falling back, both ways

What would ease it (keep prices up)

  • The Strait of Hormuz stays restricted. On Nov 10–11 Iran's forces reported tankers on fire there, one after hitting a mine (Euronews). Brent was $91.71 that morning.
  • OPEC+ stays put. On Nov 1 the seven core members kept December targets unchanged, and most Gulf members are still producing below target (Enerdata).
  • Stocks are drawn down. The EIA has world inventories falling 1.9 million barrels a day in Q3 and another 0.7 million in Q4, and expects them to rebuild only gradually in 2027.
  • Refining stays short, which holds margins up even if crude eases.

What would make it worse (push prices down)

  • A reopening. The EIA's October outlook has Brent at $105 in Q4 2026, $87 by Q2 2027 and $74 by Q4 2027, with most Middle East output back by the end of Q2 2027.
  • Brent is already under that path: about $92 on Nov 11 against the EIA's $105 for the quarter.
  • The Fed. Officials say energy is feeding core inflation; futures put about 90% odds on a December rise. Tighter money leans on demand.
  • The arithmetic, mine and rough: from the $96 in the filing to the EIA's $74 is $22, or about $13B a year after tax at $600M per dollar.

How this company is placed against it

  • It is not spending the windfall. Capex is finishing at the low end of the range, and debt is down to $33.5B from $40.8B at year-end.
  • Hedges: not found. A planning price: not found (only Occidental gave one, $50 WTI).
  • Versus peers: Exxon says product markets normalise well through next year, which fits Chevron's view that margins stay elevated near term and then ease. Occidental has capped its upside with collars; Chevron keeps the full swing in both directions.

Money plans

  • Capital spending: Q3 capex $4.0B; full year at the low end of $18–19B. 2027 capex comes in December.
  • Buybacks: 12.87M shares in Q3; $2.5–3.0B planned for Q4; $28.5B left on the programme.
  • Dividend: $1.78 a share.
  • Debt: $33.5B, down from $40.8B at year-end.

People and governance

  • CEO: Mike Wirth, chairman and CEO since February 2018, so more than eight years. Not a founder. In 2023 the independent directors waived the retirement age of 65 so he could stay.
  • CFO: Eimear Bonner, since early 2024. This changes on Jan 1, 2027: Jeff Gustavson, now president of New Energies, becomes CFO, and Bonner becomes president of oil, products and gas (announced Oct 5, 2026).
  • Successor named: Not formally. Wirth said last December he was discussing succession with the board and did not expect to serve more than 10 years. Investors told Reuters that Bonner and Gustavson are both seen as CEO candidates (BNN Bloomberg). Mark Nelson stays as vice chairman.
  • Control: No controlling holder or dual-class shares found. Chair and CEO roles are combined. Lead independent director: not found. Board of 14.
  • Recent changes or investigations: The CFO swap above. No investigation found in the last 12 months.
  • How much rests on one person: Moderate and falling. A handover is being prepared in the open, with two candidates being rotated through the big jobs. The risk is the usual one for a handover: a new CEO inside roughly two years, with a new CFO from January.
