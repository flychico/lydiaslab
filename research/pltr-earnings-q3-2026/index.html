PLTR earnings — Palantir, Q3 2026

November 2, 202612 min readOpen in the reader

Management sees more demand than it can serve and raised every line of its outlook; the thing that could spoil it is that new contract signings have stopped growing as fast as revenue.

Reported 2026-11-02. Next report: not found (early February 2027 is an outside estimate). Updated 2026-11-12. Research, not investment advice.

The short version

  • Demand: stronger. Revenue +96%; US commercial +148% and now larger than US government.
  • Guidance: raised on every line. 2026 revenue $8.530–8.534B (from $8.150–8.158B); adjusted operating income $5.245–5.249B (from $4.889–4.897B); adjusted free cash flow $4.8–5.0B (from $4.5–4.7B). Q4 revenue $2.649–2.653B.
  • Biggest worry: bookings. Contract value signed rose only 6% from a year ago, and remaining performance obligations were flat in the quarter at $4.9B.
  • Chart: leans up, narrowly. Price sits on my 38.2% line at $172.23; a close below $159.66 (the 50% line) would flip it.

The numbers

  • Revenue $2.31B (+96%) vs $2.18B estimate, and well above its own $2.160–2.164B guide.
  • EPS $0.44 (GAAP and adjusted are the same this quarter) vs $0.376 estimate. A clean beat on both.
  • Net dollar retention 165%; US commercial customers 686.

What management said about the road ahead

  • Next quarter: revenue $2.649–2.653B. Nothing on 2027.
  • Demand: ahead of what the company can serve, led by US commercial and a new "Sovereign AI" offering. "We really cannot satiate demand." (Alex Karp, CEO; third-party transcript)
  • Bookings: total contract value signed $2.92B, +6% from a year ago (it grew 61% in Q1 and 49% in Q2, per TIKR). Karp put the slowdown down to several large deals signed on shorter initial terms.
  • Backlog: remaining deal value $13.4B (+2% in the quarter); remaining performance obligations $4.9B, flat.
  • US commercial: 2026 revenue now above $3.559B (from above $3.424B).
  • International commercial: the soft spot, +1% in the quarter.
  • AI labs: described as widening Palantir's market; management was critical of software firms that resell AI usage as revenue.

What management is worried about

  1. Dependence on a new offering. The filing says growth depends on continued adoption of new products; "Sovereign AI" is carrying a large share of the story.
  2. Customer concentration. The top three customers are 16% of revenue.
  3. Contracts that can be ended for convenience. Standard in government work; it means backlog is softer than it looks.
  4. Government budget delays. US government revenue was $953M in the quarter.
  5. AI regulation.
  6. Stock-based pay. $318.9M in the quarter, +85%.

The big worry: bookings not keeping pace, both ways

What would ease it

  • Short-term deals renewing and growing. Net dollar retention of 165% says existing customers are spending far more each year.
  • Contract value signed re-accelerating in Q4. One quarter of 6% after 61% and 49% could be timing.
  • A defense budget that lands. The Army enterprise agreement runs 10 years and up to $10B, and a Pentagon memo directs up to $243.9M of no-bid Palantir spending through Mar 31, 2027 (Defense One).
  • Compute supply arriving. The AI builders you track all say demand exceeds supply, and Palantir's customers need that capacity to deploy.

What would make it worse

  • A second quarter of single-digit bookings growth while revenue guidance still assumes a near doubling.
  • A budget fight. The funding stopgap expires Dec 11, 2026, and about $350B of the $1.5T fiscal 2027 defense request depends on a bill the incoming Congress is expected to block (Defense One).
  • Short contracts not renewing. Shorter initial terms mean customers can leave sooner.
  • The price of the stock. TIKR puts it at 94 times forward earnings; at that price a flat backlog matters more than it would elsewhere.

How this company is placed against it

  • No debt, $10.4B in cash and Treasuries, capex about $30M in nine months. It does not need the credit markets, unlike the AI builders.
  • Growing 96% against ServiceNow's 20%; on the evidence so far AI is helping this vendor.
  • Against that: the largest fixed commitment is a cloud hosting bill of at least $5.6B over ten years, and the government half of the business is exposed to Washington's calendar.

Money plans

  • Capex about $30M in nine months; no guide.
  • No meaningful buybacks, no dividend, no debt. Cash and Treasuries $10.4B.
  • Headcount 4,634. Stock-based pay $318.9M in the quarter.

People and governance

  • CEO: Alex Karp, co-founder, CEO since 2005.
  • Chair: Peter Thiel, chairman since 2003. The roles are split.
  • CFO: David Glazer. Start date: not found.
  • Successor named: no; the proxy describes no succession plan.
  • Control: three share classes. Class B carries 10 votes; Class F sits in a founder voting trust that lets the founders control up to 49.999999% of the votes. Individual percentages: not found.
  • Recent changes or investigations: a securities class action against officers and directors was dismissed in April 2025 and the appeal was argued in March 2026 (10-Q). Thiel filed to sell 2M shares, about $290M, in March 2026, as reported by Benzinga. On May 20, 2026 Karp sold about $54.1M, Cohen about $43.5M and Sankar about $22.5M, described as tax-related sales on vested stock, as reported by Parameter. Departures: not found.
  • How much rests on one person: a great deal. Karp has run it for two decades, the founders hold close to half the votes whatever they sell, and nobody is named behind him.
