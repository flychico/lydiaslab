WMT earnings — Walmart, fiscal Q2 2027

August 20, 202613 min readOpen in the reader

In August management saw steady sales and a raised year, paid for with price cuts, and the thing that could spoil it is the lower-income shopper, who was already squeezed by fuel before the most expensive October for gasoline on record.

This report is old. It covers the quarter ended July 31 and was published Aug 20. Walmart reports again on Nov 19, 2026, and this page will be replaced then.

Reported Aug 20, 2026. Next report: Nov 19, 2026. Updated Nov 12, 2026. Research, not investment advice.

The short version

  • Demand: steady overall, weaker at lower incomes. US comparable sales +2.6%; online sales +23% globally.
  • Guidance: raised in August. Sales growth 4.0–5.0% (was 3.5–4.5%); adjusted EPS $2.80–2.87 (was $2.75–2.85). For the quarter due Nov 19: sales growth 3.0–3.75% and adjusted EPS $0.62–0.64.
  • Biggest worry: the squeezed lower- and middle-income shopper, with fuel as the pressure point.
  • Chart: leans up into the report. The level that decides it is the 200-day at $118.50. These are my lines, not yours.

The numbers

  • Revenue $187.9B against $186.8B expected. A beat.
  • Adjusted EPS $0.81 against about $0.74; GAAP EPS $0.80. A beat on both.
  • Tariff refunds lifted gross margin by about a point. Nearly $2.9B was received, and almost all of it is going into lower prices.

What management said about the road ahead

  • Next quarter (due Nov 19): sales growth 3.0–3.75%, operating income growth 2.0–4.0%, adjusted EPS $0.62–0.64. Webull's estimate is $0.64 and $187.2B, the top of the range.
  • Full year: adjusted operating income growth 7.0–8.5% (was 6.0–8.0%).
  • Shoppers: lower- and middle-income households were squeezed by gasoline above $4 and June was soft; higher-income households kept moving their spending to Walmart. The CFO said shoppers were buying fewer items per trip.
  • Pricing: price cuts ("rollbacks") rose to more than 11,000 from about 7,200. "We're investing heavily in price because customers need us to." John Furner, President and CEO.
  • Inflation: grocery 1.3%, general merchandise 1.7%.
  • Costs: guidance assumes more than $2B of extra fuel cost and a larger drag from government drug-price rules.

What management is worried about

  1. Fuel prices. More than $2B of extra cost in guidance, on top of what fuel does to the shopper.
  2. Consumer trade-offs. US comparable sales of +2.6% were called the slowest in six years by trade press, mainly because of pharmacy.
  3. Drug-price regulation. A larger drag on pharmacy sales; no figure given.
  4. Tariffs. New or changed tariffs remain a named risk; the refunds are a one-off.
  5. Spending more to stand still. Capital spending rises to about 4.0% of sales (was about 3.5%).

The big worry: the squeezed lower-income shopper, both ways

What would ease it

  • Fuel coming down. AAA's national average was $3.94 on Nov 11, against $4.12 a week earlier and $4.36 a month earlier.
  • Mood turning. University of Michigan sentiment rose to 51.0 in early November from 48.0, and it rose for lower- and middle-wealth households.
  • Jobs holding. Payrolls +165,000 in October with unemployment at 4.1% (Fed desk doc). Visa and JPMorgan both said in October that lower-end card spending had not weakened.
  • Trade-down. When money is tight, higher-income households shop at Walmart more; Dollar General reported the same pattern in August.

What would make it worse

  • The quarter being reported was the bad stretch. October gasoline averaged $4.31, the highest October on record and 41% above a year earlier, with diesel at $6.25 (EIA figures, via a Nov 9 article). The relief only arrived this month.
  • Oil turning back up. The themes doc has WTI near $87 after a tanker struck a mine in the Strait of Hormuz, and the oil companies in this project describe restrictions that are not yet resolved.
  • Food benefits shrinking. Circana estimates about 2 million fewer households on SNAP and about $10B less benefit spending in 2026 than 2025.
  • Rates. December is priced for another Fed hike, and the Beige Book says customers are resisting price increases.
  • Others report the same strain. McDonald's US sales fell 0.2% last quarter with the low-income customer named as the reason; Dollar Tree's CEO said customers are managing budgets carefully.

How this company is placed against it

  • It planned for it: the guidance already carries $2B of fuel cost, and the tariff refunds were spent on price, not margin.
  • It gains from trade-down, and grocery is the last thing households cut. The weak spot is general merchandise and pharmacy.
  • Rivals are cutting too. Target made more than 10,000 price cuts over the past year, so some of Walmart's price spending only holds its place.

Money plans

  • Capital spending $14.2B in six months, up from $11.4B; about 4.0% of sales for the year.
  • Buybacks $5.1B year to date; $25.1B authorised.
  • Dividend $0.99 a share for the year.
  • Total debt $57.2B.

People and governance

  • CEO: John Furner, since Feb 1, 2026. Promoted from running Walmart U.S.; not a founder.
  • Previous CEO: Doug McMillon retired Jan 31, 2026, left the board in June, and is due to advise Furner until Jan 31, 2027.
  • CFO: John David Rainey, since 2022.
  • Successor named: not found.
  • Control: one class of stock, but entities tied to the Walton family own about 44% (2026 proxy). That is effective control without dual-class shares.
  • Chair: Greg Penner, non-executive and not counted as independent. Lead independent director: Randall Stephenson.
  • Recent changes: a broad reshuffle under the new CEO. David Guggina took over Walmart U.S.; the International chief, Kathryn McLay, is leaving and Chris Nicholas replaced her; Latriece Watkins moved to run Sam's Club. CNBC reported in May, citing internal memos, that two more senior operators were leaving. Investigations of executives: not found.
  • How much rests on one person: not much; the family stake and a deep bench carry it. The open question is a new CEO and several new division heads meeting a harder consumer in the same year.

  • Momentum: RSI 63.7, firm.
  • Ceiling: $131.82 (the Nov 5 high), then the 52-week high of $134.83 from May 19.
  • Floor: $120.96 (38.2% of the Oct 5 low $103.39 to the Nov 5 high), then the 200-day at $118.50, then $114.25 (61.8%).
  • What the run means: the stock rose about 27% from the Oct 5 low to the Nov 5 high, ahead of the report. Much of a good quarter is already in the price.

  • Scorecard: no open or watching play on WMT.
  • Verdict: the chart leans up, which is more confident than what management said in August about the lower-income shopper. The two disagree, and Nov 19 settles it. A close below $118.50 would flip the chart down; a close above $131.82 would confirm it. The lines are mine; you have not drawn WMT.

What would change this read

  • The Nov 19 report: US comparable sales against +2.6%, what management says about lower-income households after the October fuel spike, and whether the full-year range is held.
  • Gasoline holding under $4, or going back over it.
  • A close below $118.50 or above $131.82.
