FOMC Minutes — July 28–29, 2026

July 29, 20263 min readOpen in the reader

Decision

With Kevin Warsh chairing, the Committee held the federal funds range at 3.50–3.75%. IORB stayed at 3.65% and the primary credit rate at 3.75%. The vote was 9–3. Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan dissented because they wanted a 25bp hike. The minutes note that participants favoring a hike saw price pressures as broad-based and judged that a more restrictive stance was needed to meet the Fed's goals on a sustained basis.

Economic outlook

Staff expected GDP to grow slightly faster than potential next year. Unemployment was seen staying near its longer-run rate and then dipping slightly below it by 2028. Staff saw growth and employment risks skewed to the downside.

Inflation

Headline PCE was 4.1% in May and core was 3.4%. Staff estimated June eased to 3.7% headline and 3.3% core. Participants called the price increases broad-based, pointing to core services excluding housing and to data-center inputs such as chips and steel. Short-term survey expectations had risen since the Middle East conflict began, while longer-term expectations stayed consistent with 2%. Most participants expected inflation to ease later this year, but many warned it could persist, and "inflation risks were skewed to the upside." Staff saw inflation reaching about 2% in 2028.

Labor market

Unemployment was 4.2% in June, little changed over two years. Average hourly earnings rose 3.5% over the past year. Participants saw a stable labor market that was strengthening modestly, with low layoffs and jobless claims. Soft spots included a low job-finding rate and elevated long-term unemployment. Strong demand for AI-skilled workers pushed up their wages.

Financial markets & balance sheet

Oil rose after Middle East tensions escalated. Nominal Treasury yields climbed 25–30bp, mostly because of higher real rates. Markets fully priced a 25bp hike by September and another by the end of Q1 2027. The Desk's survey median, by contrast, expected no change this year or next. The S&P 500 was roughly flat to slightly lower, and AI-infrastructure leaders stalled. Credit spreads on hyperscaler debt widened, and business development company redemptions kept rising. The dollar rose, with heavy foreign inflows into U.S. stocks. Balance sheet operations were unchanged. Most participants wanted a broad future discussion of balance sheet policy informed by a task force, and many reaffirmed that interest rates remain the main policy tool.

Risks participants flagged

  • Staff rated financial vulnerabilities as notable. The equity risk premium was near dot-com-era lows, and hedge fund leverage was near record highs.
  • AI-related repricing, and AI financing through nonbank lenders and regional banks
  • Private credit weakness, Treasury market volatility and AI-linked cyber threats
  • The Middle East conflict
  • A sharp AI-driven stock selloff that could tighten financial conditions

What it signaled about next moves

Most participants preferred to wait for more data, but the bias was toward tightening. Many said tightening would likely be needed if inflation failed to fall. Some doubted that financial conditions were restrictive enough. A few argued that hiking early could avoid a costlier tightening later. Warsh also floated cutting the number of meetings from eight a year to six, though nothing was decided and the 2026 calendar was unchanged.

Read-through for SPY

  • Three hawkish dissents and markets pricing in hikes meant rising rates were now the base case, a direct headwind for valuations.
  • The Fed noted the equity risk premium was near dot-com lows. Stocks offered little cushion over bonds, so an AI-led repricing was a named risk.
  • Widening hyperscaler credit spreads and stalling AI-infrastructure stocks suggested market leadership was getting fragile.
