FOMC Minutes — June 17–18, 2025

June 18, 20253 min readOpen in the reader

Official minutes: HTML · PDF Local PDF: 2025-06-18 FOMC Minutes.pdf

Decision

The Committee held the federal funds target range at 4-1/4 to 4-1/2 percent for a fourth straight meeting, with interest on reserve balances at 4.4 percent. The vote was unanimous, 12-0: Powell, Williams, Barr, Bowman, Collins, Cook, Goolsbee, Jefferson, Kugler, Musalem, Schmid and Waller. Runoff continued with caps of $5 billion for Treasuries and $35 billion for agency debt and MBS. This was a Summary of Economic Projections meeting, with projections through 2027 released afterward (figures not in the minutes).

Economic outlook

Staff said real GDP was rising in the second quarter after a slight first-quarter decline, and raised its growth projection through 2027 from May, mainly because it assumed lower effective tariff rates. Participants described growth as solid, though a majority expected it to moderate. Consumer spending and business investment were solid, but household and business sentiment remained weak.

Inflation

Total PCE inflation was estimated at 2.3 percent and core at 2.6 percent over the 12 months to May, both lower than at the start of the year. Short-term survey expectations remained high, though some eased in May and June; longer-term expectations were steady and well anchored. Staff expected tariffs to raise inflation this year, add a small boost in 2026, and see inflation back at 2 percent by 2027, with upside risks.

Labor market

Unemployment was 4.2 percent in May, unchanged for three months. Payrolls rose at a solid pace, slightly above the prior four-month average. The vacancies-to-unemployed ratio held at 1.0 and average hourly earnings rose 3.9 percent over the year. Participants judged the labor market at or near maximum employment, but many expected gradual softening, and immigration policy was cited as reducing labor supply.

Financial markets & balance sheet

Treasury yields rose 15 to 20 basis points over the period, credit spreads narrowed and the dollar fell further. Reserves were nearly $3.5 trillion, "well into the abundant range," helped by the debt limit drawing down the Treasury General Account by nearly $420 billion. Once the debt limit is resolved, the TGA rebuild was expected to drain liquidity quickly. SOMA holdings had fallen almost $2-1/4 trillion since June 2022; survey respondents expected runoff to end in February 2026 with the portfolio near $6.2 trillion and reserves near $2.9 trillion. The Desk added morning standing repo operations starting June 26.

Risks participants flagged

  • Tariffs could raise inflation more, or for longer, than expected and could weigh on hiring; pass-through to consumers was uncertain.
  • Fiscal, immigration and regulatory policy changes added uncertainty.
  • Middle East developments were an upside risk to energy prices, though staff saw limited effects from the Israel-Iran conflict beyond energy.
  • Student loan delinquencies were expected to rise.
  • Persistent inflation alongside weakening employment would create a difficult policy tradeoff.

What it signaled about next moves

Most participants judged that some reduction in the target range this year would likely be appropriate. A couple were open to cutting as soon as the next meeting if data evolved as expected, while some saw no cuts this year. Several thought the current rate may not be far above neutral. All agreed the Committee was well positioned to wait for more clarity.

Read-through for SPY

  • The first clear signal that most officials expected cuts in 2025, a meaningful tailwind for stock valuations.
  • Upgraded growth projections reduced recession risk, supporting the earnings outlook.
  • The split between early cutters and no-cut participants meant rate-sensitive stocks would stay data-dependent.
