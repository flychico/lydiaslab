AAPL earnings — Apple, fiscal Q4 2026

November 2, 202613 min readOpen in the reader

Management sees demand it cannot fully supply and is raising prices to keep up; the thing that could spoil it is the cost of memory chips, which it expects to keep climbing.

Reported 2026-11-02. Next report: about Jan 28, 2027 (unconfirmed estimate). Updated 2026-11-12. Research, not investment advice.

The short version

  • Demand: stronger, but limited by supply. iPhone revenue +17%, Greater China +21%.
  • Guidance: one quarter only, and a step down from what was just delivered. December-quarter revenue growth 7–10% (it just grew 12%); gross margin 45–46% (it just made 47.9%); Services growth 8–9%.
  • Biggest worry: memory costs, called unprecedented by the CEO and expected to keep rising after December.
  • Chart: leans up. Price closed at a record; a close below $334.20 (my 38.2% line) would flip it.

The numbers

  • Revenue $114.48B (+12%) vs $113.35B estimate. EPS $2.10 (+14%) vs $1.98 estimate. Both beat.
  • Read the quality: EPS includes $0.05 from tariff refunds, and the 47.9% gross margin includes about 1 point from them. Without the refunds the margin was about 46.9%.
  • This filing is the annual 10-K. Fiscal 2026 capex was $9.0B, down from $12.7B.

What management said about the road ahead

  • Next quarter: revenue growth 7–10%, explicitly held back by supply; gross margin 45–46%; operating expenses $20.4–20.8B.
  • Memory: costs are higher in the December quarter and expected to keep rising after it, only partly offset by price increases. "What we're seeing with memory pricing right now is unprecedented." (John Ternus, CEO; third-party transcript)
  • Supply: the limit was advanced chips last quarter and is memory for Mac and iPad this quarter.
  • Pricing: Apple has raised prices on iPhone, iPad and Mac and said it does not yet know how buyers will respond.
  • Services: slowing, 10% growth going to 8–9%. Server costs for the new AI Siri will sit in Services costs.
  • Tariff refunds: the CFO said the vast majority have now been received (ithinkdiff recap), so that help fades.
  • Installed base: active devices above 2.5 billion.

What management is worried about

  1. Memory cost and supply. About 1 point off gross margin between the June and September quarters already, and a guide roughly 2 points lower for December.
  2. How buyers react to higher prices. Management said plainly it does not know yet.
  3. Advanced-chip supply and limited third-party computing capacity for AI.
  4. Tariffs and refund uncertainty. Refunds were worth $0.05 of EPS this quarter.
  5. Legal and regulatory: App Store rulings, and the Google search-licensing case, where both sides have appealed.
  6. Manufacturing concentration in Asia.

The big worry: memory costs, both ways

What would ease it

  • Memory price rises slowing. Micron said it expects more moderate increases after its current quarter.
  • Weaker PC and phone demand elsewhere. AMD and Micron both expect PC and phone unit sales to fall by double digits next year, which frees supply for the buyers who remain.
  • Long-term supply deals. Apple reportedly signed a multi-year NAND agreement, with Kioxia seen as the likely partner (unconfirmed; TrendForce citing Economy Tribune).
  • Price increases sticking. Apple raised Mac prices by $100 to $500 and iPad prices by $150 to $200 in June, and the iPhone 18 Pro models by $100 in September.

What would make it worse

  • Supply staying tight for years. Micron expects memory to be much tighter in 2027 and 2028 than in 2026 and says it cannot see when supply and demand balance.
  • Contract prices still climbing. TrendForce put fourth-quarter contract prices up 10–15% for standard DRAM and 15–20% for NAND against the third quarter.
  • AI builders outbidding consumer devices. Nvidia's supply commitments more than doubled to $279B in a quarter, mostly memory; Dell, AMD, Broadcom and Amazon all named memory as a rising cost.
  • Buyers balking at higher prices. The Fed is watching exactly this: October CPI showed smartphone prices +1.4% on the month, and its business contacts report customers resisting increases.

How this company is placed against it

  • Better than most hardware makers: it is the largest buyer, it has pricing power, and it kept a gross margin near 47% while Dell runs near 21%.
  • Not immune: the supply deal reported for it carries no price cap, and TrendForce estimates memory cost for the iPhone 18 Pro (256GB) was nearly four times last year's level in the third quarter.
  • Cash is not the issue: about $24B of buybacks in one quarter. Management has, though, dropped its long-standing net-cash-neutral target.

Money plans

  • Buybacks about $24B in the quarter and about $86B in the year; $114.0B of authorisation left. Dividend $0.27 a share.
  • Capex $9.0B in fiscal 2026, down from $12.7B; no fiscal 2027 guide found. R&D $45.8B, +32%.
  • The net-cash-neutral target was dropped. Headcount about 167,000.

People and governance

  • CEO: John Ternus, since Sept 1, 2026. Not a founder; joined Apple in 2001 and ran hardware engineering. This was his first earnings report as CEO.
  • Chair: Tim Cook, executive chairman since Sept 1, 2026 (the change was announced Apr 20). The roles are split, but the chair is the former CEO, not an independent director. Arthur Levinson moved from chairman to lead independent director.
  • CFO: Kevan Parekh, since the start of 2025.
  • Successor named: this was the succession, announced more than four months ahead. A successor to Ternus: not found.
  • Control: no controlling holder or dual-class shares found.
  • Recent changes or investigations: a wide turnover at the top. Jeff Williams left at the end of 2025 (Sabih Khan is chief operating officer); the general counsel and the environment chief retired. Bloomberg's Gurman, as relayed by iClarified, reports that hardware chief Johny Srouji may consider leaving within one to two years and that two other senior executives are expected to leave within a few years. Investigations of leadership: not found.
  • How much rests on one person: less than at most companies you track. The handover was planned and the former CEO is still in the room. The risk is the opposite kind: a new CEO, a new operating chief and more departures reported to be coming, all at once.
